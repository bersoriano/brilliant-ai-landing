As financial institutions introduce new restrictions on independent AI agents, an important question emerges: who will control the future of personal finance?

Imagine waking up on a Monday morning and receiving a message from your personal AI assistant:

> “I’ve reviewed your finances. You’re paying for three subscriptions you rarely use, your savings could be earning more interest elsewhere, and I’ve identified a better way to manage your credit card payments. Would you like me to take care of it?”

It sounds like a natural evolution of artificial intelligence. After all, if AI can write software, organize our schedules and plan our trips, why shouldn’t it help us manage our money?

Some of the world’s largest financial institutions, however, are beginning to set boundaries.

## Banks are drawing a line

On November 16, 2026, American Express updated its [U.S. Terms of Service](https://www.americanexpress.com/us/legal-disclosures/website-rules-and-regulations.html) to explicitly address the use of artificial intelligence agents across its digital platforms.

The updated terms restrict unauthorized automated access, credential sharing and interactions between independent AI agents and customer accounts. American Express also reserves the right to restrict account functionality when these rules are violated.

| AI agent activity | How the U.S. terms apply |
| --- | --- |
| Summarize or compare public Amex offers | A qualifying public answer service may cite or briefly summarize public pages in response to a user request, subject to the terms. |
| Assist with accessibility in a signed-in session | Permitted when the tool helps the authorized user access or use the site. |
| Give an agent your Amex login credentials | Prohibited. Supported providers use an authorized access mechanism. |
| Scrape Amex site content for AI training | The permissions for public search and answer services exclude AI training. |
| Have an agent retrieve transactions, manage rewards, or change settings | Requires an Amex-authorized way to access the account and perform the action. |
| Have an agent make purchases | May be possible through an expressly authorized interface or process. |

And American Express is not alone.

JPMorgan Chase has introduced requirements for AI agents to identify themselves and respect its access restrictions. Wells Fargo has updated its online access agreement to address customer responsibilities and potential liability when using AI agents.

Other financial institutions are moving in a similar direction. Reuters reported in October 2026 that major US banks were already blocking unauthorized third-party AI agents from operating inside customer accounts.

What makes this particularly interesting is that many of these same institutions are actively investing in artificial intelligence.

They are not necessarily saying no to AI.

They are saying that AI must interact with their systems on terms they recognize and control.

## From open banking to agentic banking

Over the past two decades, the way we interact with financial institutions has changed dramatically.

First came digital banking, allowing us to manage our money without visiting a branch.

Then came open banking, enabling third-party applications to connect to financial institutions, consolidate account information and provide services beyond those offered by individual banks.

Now, we are entering another stage: agentic banking.

The difference is relatively simple.

- **Digital banking:** You manage your finances online.
- **Open banking:** Applications help you connect and understand your finances.
- **Agentic banking:** AI agents help you make decisions and potentially act on your behalf.

Rather than opening several banking applications, reviewing transactions and comparing financial products yourself, you could delegate much of that work to a personal AI assistant.

Your relationship with banking could shift from performing individual tasks to defining financial objectives.

Instead of telling an application what to do, you tell your agent what you want to achieve.

## Why are banks concerned?

There are perfectly legitimate reasons for financial institutions to be cautious.

An AI agent could misunderstand an instruction, transfer an incorrect amount of money, expose sensitive information or perform an action its owner never intended.

When real money is involved, the consequences are considerably more serious than an AI generating an inaccurate email.

There is also the question of responsibility. If an AI agent makes a financial mistake, who should be accountable? The customer, the bank or the company that developed the agent?

But beyond security and liability, there is another interesting dimension: the relationship between banks and their customers.

Imagine an independent AI assistant that continuously compares financial products across different institutions. It identifies better savings rates, lower fees, more attractive credit card benefits and cheaper international transfers.

Unlike a traditional banking application, this assistant would not necessarily have any loyalty to a particular financial institution.

Its loyalty could be entirely to you.

If millions of consumers adopted such assistants, financial institutions might find themselves competing more directly on the quality and value of their products rather than benefiting from customers’ reluctance to switch.

The competition would no longer be exclusively about who has the best banking application.

It could become about who offers the best financial outcome.

## What about people who want to build their own AI agents?

For technology enthusiasts, independent developers and privacy-conscious consumers, these restrictions raise an interesting question about personal autonomy.

If it is your money, your account, your financial information and your computer, should you be able to develop your own AI assistant to manage them?

Today, someone with relatively modest technical knowledge can build a private AI assistant capable of analyzing expenses, identifying unnecessary fees, forecasting cash flow and generating personalized financial recommendations.

Much of this can even be done locally, without sending sensitive financial information to a commercial AI provider.

The challenge appears when that assistant needs to communicate directly with financial institutions.

**Building the intelligence is becoming easier. Obtaining permission to use it is becoming more complicated.**

For now, independent AI assistants can still provide considerable value by working with downloaded statements, authorized financial connections and information voluntarily supplied by users.

But the ability to independently automate financial operations will largely depend on how banks choose to open their systems.

## A ban on AI, or the beginning of a new ecosystem?

American Express provides an interesting example of this apparent contradiction.

Earlier in 2026, the company introduced its Agentic Commerce Experiences developer kit, designed to enable registered AI agents to make authorized purchases on behalf of cardholders.

In other words, American Express is simultaneously restricting unauthorized AI agents and building infrastructure to support authorized ones.

This suggests that the future of AI-powered finance may not be about preventing automation altogether.

It may be about establishing a new framework for trust, identity, permissions and accountability.

The question is whether that framework will be accessible to everyone or primarily to large technology companies and approved commercial partners.

## Three possible futures

As agentic banking develops, we could see three different models emerge.

### Closed agentic banking

Each financial institution offers its own AI assistant. Customers benefit from intelligent services but remain within individual banking ecosystems.

### Partner-based agentic banking

Financial institutions allow selected AI providers to connect to their services. Customers gain more flexibility, although their choice of assistant remains limited to approved partners.

### Open agentic banking

Consumers can choose, develop or host their own AI assistants and securely authorize them to interact with participating financial institutions within clearly defined limits.

These models could coexist, but the third represents a particularly interesting opportunity for consumer independence and technological innovation.

It would bring the original spirit of open banking into the age of artificial intelligence.

## The future of financial freedom

The next generation of financial technology should not simply make banks more intelligent. It should empower individuals to make better financial decisions.

Security, privacy and innovation do not have to be competing objectives. With appropriate safeguards, consumers could delegate financial responsibilities while maintaining control over their money, information and choice of technology.

At Brilliant AI, we see this as part of a much broader transformation in software: the movement from applications that people operate to intelligent systems that work on their behalf.

Banking is only one example, but it may become one of the most consequential.

The real question is no longer whether artificial intelligence will manage aspects of our financial lives.

It is whose interests that intelligence will represent.

> Perhaps the future of banking isn’t having an intelligent bank. It’s having your own intelligence that can interact with any bank.

## Next step: map where your bank data comes from

List the bank and card portals your team logs into each week to pull statements, reconcile accounts or schedule payments. For each one, note how the data reaches you today (a downloaded statement, an authorized connection, or someone copying numbers by hand) and what the current terms say about automated or AI-agent access.

Where only a person may log in, an agent can still prepare the work from the statements your team downloads, while the login and the payment approval stay with that person. If you want to try that on one account, a 20-minute workflow review is usually enough to see whether it’s worth piloting.
