**Who this is for:** controllers, AP leads and practice managers who want people to keep approving the work, without approvals becoming the slowest step.

<Takeaway>
An approval is only as fast as the card it arrives on. Ask one question per card, bring the evidence to it, name the approver and a backup, and agree in writing which items need a person at all.
</Takeaway>

“Human in the loop” is easy to promise and easy to get wrong. If every item waits on someone who has to open three systems before saying yes, you haven’t removed the bottleneck. You’ve moved it.

In finance and healthcare, the approval is the point. Recording, paying, scheduling and anything clinical stay with people. So the approval deserves as much thought as the automation around it. Here are six rules that keep approvals fast, based on where we keep seeing them slow down.

## 1. One approval, one decision

Each card asks one question:

- record this entry,
- send this follow-up,
- mark this referral ready to schedule.

If a reviewer has to make two decisions, it’s two cards.

## 2. Bring the evidence to the approval

The card should carry everything needed to decide. If the reviewer has to open another tab, something is missing.

- **An invoice:** the invoice, the purchase order and the difference.
- **A referral:** the referral and the fields that are missing.
- **A message:** the draft and who it’s going to.

## 3. Decide in writing what needs a person

Not everything does, and pretending otherwise floods the queue. Agree on the line before go-live. Then look at it again after the pilot, with the real exceptions in front of you.

| Item | Can wait in a batch for one approval | Gets its own card |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Supplier invoice | Matches its PO within tolerance | A price, quantity or vendor mismatch |
| Referral | Complete, including insurance details | Missing insurance details: it goes back to the referring practice with a drafted request, for your team to send |
| Message to a client or patient | A reminder from an approved template (a written rule can let it go out on its own) | Any new message: drafted for a person to send |

These are examples. Where you draw the line depends on your tolerances and how much risk you’re comfortable with.

## 4. Name the approver, and the backup

An approval assigned to “finance” waits. One assigned to the AP lead, with a named backup for the days they’re out, gets cleared.

## 5. Put approvals where people already work

Teams, Outlook, the intake queue. A new dashboard is one more place to check, and usually the last one anyone checks.

## 6. Keep an audit trail of every approval

Who approved, when, and what they saw. That record is what lets a controller or a practice manager trust the workflow. It’s also what you’ll need the day someone asks why.

## In healthcare, some decisions never reach the workflow

Clinical triage, care decisions, coding changes and the rules for contacting patients stay with qualified staff. The workflow only handles the administrative routing around them:

- collecting the referral,
- checking the required fields,
- drafting the follow-up for review.

## Checklist: approval design before go-live

- Each card asks one question.
- The evidence is on the card.
- The line between “batch” and “its own card” is written down.
- Every approval has a named owner and a named backup.
- Cards arrive in Teams, Outlook or the queue your team already uses.
- Every approval is logged: who, when, and what they saw.

Get these right and people stay in control without becoming the slowest step in the process.

## Next step: time one approval from start to finish

Pick one approval your team gives every day. Time it from the moment the item arrives to the moment someone says yes, and count how many systems they open on the way. If it’s more than one, start with rule 2.

If you want to walk through that approval with someone, a 20-minute workflow review is usually enough to see whether it’s worth piloting.
