**Who this is for:** owners and managers of small businesses, from a ten-person firm to a growing practice, deciding whether they need a CRM, an ERP, or both.

<Takeaway>
A CRM is how you win and keep customers; an ERP is how you run the rest of the business. Start with the system that fixes your biggest problem, and compare license costs over five years, not one, before you sign.
</Takeaway>

If you run a small business, you’ve probably heard both terms, and maybe felt pressure to buy “the software” that will finally organize everything. The reality is simpler than most vendors make it sound.

- **CRM (customer relationship management)** is how you win and keep customers.
- **ERP (enterprise resource planning)** is how you run the rest of the business without chaos.

They solve different problems. Most growing companies eventually need both, or one system that does both well.

## What’s the difference between ERP and CRM?

Think of your business as a restaurant.

- **CRM is the front of the house:** greeting people, taking orders, remembering regulars, following up, and turning one-time visitors into loyal customers. It tracks leads, deals, conversations and support issues.
- **ERP is the kitchen and back office:** ingredient inventory, supplier orders, staff schedules, costs, invoices, and making sure you don’t run out of cash or product.

One focuses on revenue and relationships. The other focuses on operations, efficiency and control.

| Aspect | CRM | ERP |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary focus | Customers & relationships | Internal operations & resources |
| Main users | Sales, marketing, customer service | Finance, operations, inventory, HR |
| Core job | Track leads, deals, interactions | Manage money, inventory, orders, production |
| Main goal | Grow revenue and improve retention | Reduce costs, improve efficiency, stay compliant |
| Typical trigger | “We’re losing track of customers/leads” | “Spreadsheets and disconnected tools are breaking” |

## Common CRM and ERP options for small businesses

### CRM (customer-facing tools)

- **HubSpot**: free tier; combines marketing and sales.
- **Zoho CRM**: lower price point with a broad feature set.
- **Pipedrive**: built around the sales pipeline.
- **Salesforce**: extremely powerful, but often overkill (and pricey) for true small businesses.

### ERP (operations and back office)

- **Microsoft Dynamics 365 Business Central**: common among companies already in the Microsoft ecosystem.
- **NetSuite**: cloud platform built to scale with growth.
- **SAP Business One**: traditional option for product-based businesses.

Many of these work well, but they come with recurring per-user fees and limits on how deeply you can change the system.

## Open-source CRM and ERP: customized, without per-user licenses

Some small businesses go another way: an open-source CRM or ERP, customized to how they work. The license is free, which changes the long-term math. The work moves to implementation and upkeep instead.

### What the open-source options look like

They range from full modular suites (CRM, accounting, inventory, HR, e-commerce, manufacturing) to mature CRM-only projects and lightweight systems for smaller teams. The software is the starting point. What makes any of them fit a business is how it’s implemented and customized around the way that business works.

### Off-the-shelf vs. customized open source: the trade-offs

Neither option is better across the board. Here’s how they usually compare:

| Aspect | Off-the-shelf (proprietary) | Customized open source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| License | Per user, every month, for as long as you use it | Free; you pay for hosting, implementation and support |
| Changing the system | You configure within the vendor’s rules | You (or a partner) can change the system itself, because you have the source code |
| Data and hosting | Usually on the vendor’s cloud | You self-host or pick your own host |
| Switching later | Depends on the vendor’s export tools and contract terms | You can change hosts or partners without a vendor’s permission |
| Support | The vendor, often with a service-level agreement (SLA) | The community and implementation partners |
| Technical work | Mostly handled by the vendor | Handled by you, or by a partner you hire |

Open source tends to fit when your processes are different enough that you keep working around the software, or when per-user fees grow faster than the team. Off-the-shelf tends to fit when your processes are fairly standard and you’d rather not own the technical side.

### Proprietary vs. open-source license costs

List prices for common proprietary platforms, against the license cost of open source:

| System | List price | 10 users / year | 20 users / year | Covers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Salesforce Starter Suite[^salesforce] | $25/user/mo | ~$3,000 | ~$6,000 | CRM only |
| Salesforce Pro Suite[^salesforce] | $100/user/mo | ~$12,000 | ~$24,000 | CRM only |
| Dynamics 365 Business Central Essentials[^bc] | $80/user/mo | ~$9,600 | ~$19,200 | ERP |
| Dynamics 365 Business Central Premium[^bc] | $110/user/mo | ~$13,200 | ~$26,400 | ERP + manufacturing |
| NetSuite[^netsuite] | ~$999/mo base + $129–$199/user/mo | ~$27,000–$36,000 | ~$43,000–$60,000 | ERP + CRM; quote-based |
| Open-source CRM or ERP[^hosting] | $0 license | ~$600–$2,400 hosting | ~$600–$2,400 hosting | You pay for hosting, implementation, support |

Over five years, for a 20-user company:

- **Dynamics 365 Business Central Essentials:** ~$96,000 in licenses.
- **NetSuite:** ~$215,000–$300,000 in licenses.
- **Open source:** $0 in licenses, plus roughly $3,000–$12,000 in hosting.

That’s before implementation or customization, which every option needs.

### What open source still costs

The software is free, but implementation, customization, hosting and ongoing maintenance still cost money or time. You’ll need some in-house technical skill or a reliable partner. Support usually comes from the community or a partner, not a big vendor’s SLA. Done well, the total cost of ownership can come in well below Salesforce, NetSuite or similar platforms, with more control. Done badly, you’ve traded license fees for a system nobody can maintain.

## When a custom CRM or ERP makes sense (and when it doesn’t)

Off-the-shelf tools work well for most early-stage businesses, and for plenty of established ones. When generic software stops fitting, the signs are usually easy to spot:

- someone exports to a spreadsheet every week to do what the system can’t,
- “the software won’t let us” keeps coming up,
- you’re paying for a higher tier mainly to unlock one or two features,
- customer and financial data live in tools that don’t talk to each other.

If that sounds familiar, a customized system (open source or otherwise) is worth pricing out. If it doesn’t, a standard product is usually the simpler choice.

## CRM or ERP first? Five rules for busy owners

1. **Start with the pain.** Losing leads → CRM. Operational and financial chaos → ERP.
2. **Don’t buy both at once if you don’t need to.** Many companies grow into the second system.
3. **If cost and control matter, look at open source early.** Some open-source suites cover both CRM and ERP, which can suit product or service businesses with inventory and accounting needs.
4. **Integrate early.** The real value shows up when customer data and operational and financial data talk to each other.
5. **Look at customization (open source or otherwise) when workarounds pile up.** Constant spreadsheet exports and “the software won’t let us” are clear signals.

You don’t need the most expensive system. You need to be clear about the problem, and to pick something that removes friction instead of adding it. That can be a standard SaaS product, a customized open-source system or a custom build. The right one is whichever your team will actually use.

## Next step: write down what breaks most often

Before you look at any vendor, list the three things that most often go wrong in a normal week: a lead nobody followed up, an invoice entered twice, stock you thought you had. If most are about customers, start with a CRM. If most are about money, stock or orders, start with an ERP.
