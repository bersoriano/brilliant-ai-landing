Invoice automation usually gets pitched as “touchless” processing. Then you look at a real accounts-payable inbox. The invoices that match their purchase order (PO) and goods receipt were never the slow part. The time goes into the ones that don’t:

- a price that changed,
- a partial delivery,
- a vendor that isn’t in the vendor master,
- a PO that was closed early.

If the automation only handles the clean case, it looks great in a demo and changes very little in the week. Here’s the pattern we keep seeing: where it stalls, and what usually gets it moving.

## Five reasons AP automation stalls

**1. The easy match gets automated first.** Matching a clean invoice to a clean PO is the easiest thing to build, so it gets built first. It’s also the part your team already clears in seconds.

**2. Nobody wrote down the tolerances.** Is a $12 freight difference an exception? What about 2% over the PO? If the answer lives in one person’s head, the workflow flags everything or nothing. Either way, people stop paying attention to it.

**3. The vendor master is out of date.** Matching against stale vendor records produces false exceptions. A few of those and the team stops trusting the flags.

**4. The output lands somewhere new.** A dashboard nobody opens is just a second inbox. Approvers keep working where they always have, and the automation’s work sits there.

**5. Posting and payment get automated before anyone trusts it.** One wrong entry is enough for a controller to switch the whole thing off. Honestly, they’d be right to.

## Invoice exception handling: design for the exceptions first

Before building anything, list the handful of reasons an invoice can’t be approved today. Each one should reach a person as a clear question, with the evidence attached.

| Exception | What the approver sees | The question they answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Price mismatch | Invoice price, PO price and the difference | Accept the new price, or send it back to the vendor? |
| Quantity mismatch or partial delivery | Quantity invoiced vs. quantity received | Pay for what arrived, or hold the invoice? |
| Missing goods receipt | The PO and who ordered it | Confirm receipt, or hold? |
| Unknown vendor | The invoice and the closest match in the vendor master | Add the vendor, or reject the invoice? |
| Closed PO | The PO, when it closed and why | Reopen the PO, or reject the invoice? |

Your list will look different. Start from the reasons invoices actually waited last month, not from a feature list.

## Write the matching tolerances down

Price, quantity, freight, tax: agree each tolerance with the controller, in writing, before anything gets built. Something like: “Freight up to $25 over the PO gets approved in a batch. Anything above that gets its own card.” (That figure is just an example; set your own.)

When a tolerance changes, it changes in one place, and everyone can see the new number.

## Send a packet, not a link

The approver should see the invoice, the PO, the receipt and the difference on one card, in Teams or Outlook, and decide right there. If they have to open the ERP to answer the question, the card is missing something. [Approval workflow design that doesn’t create a new bottleneck](/blog/approval-points-without-bottlenecks) goes into how to build those cards.

## Keep posting and payment with your team

Let the workflow prepare the entry. A person you name records it, and payment stays under your control.

That’s a design choice, not a technical limit. One wrong posting costs more trust than a month of correct ones earns. The same rule applies to AI agents: they prepare, and a person decides. More on that in [AI agents in finance and healthcare: drafts, not decisions](/blog/agents-in-regulated-work).

## Run the pilot next to your current process

During the pilot, put the workflow’s output next to your team’s work on the same invoices.

- When the two agree often enough, widen the scope.
- When they don’t, you’ve found a rule that wasn’t clear, and you found it before anyone depended on the workflow.

## Checklist: what working invoice automation looks like

- Approvers decide from one card, without opening another system.
- Every exception arrives with a reason a person can read.
- Tolerances are written down and changed in one place.
- Your team can see what the workflow handled and what it sent to them.
- Nothing is recorded or paid without a named approver.

To tell whether it’s working, count hours, not a “touchless rate”: [Measuring automation ROI: hours reclaimed, without fake precision](/blog/measuring-capacity-reclaimed).

## Next step: sort last month’s exceptions by reason

Pull the invoices that waited more than a day last month and note why each one waited. Sort the reasons by how often they came up. The top three are where an invoice workflow should start, and the list doubles as a first draft of your tolerances.

If you’d like a second pair of eyes on it, bring that list to a 20-minute workflow review. It’s usually enough to see whether an invoice workflow is worth piloting.
